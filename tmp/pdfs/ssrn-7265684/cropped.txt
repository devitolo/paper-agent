I. THE AI PARADOX - WHEN
INVESTMENTS FAIL TO SCALE
Artificial intelligence is transforming companies faster
than any technology before. Most fail at scaling. Around
80 percent are experimenting with generative AI; only a
fraction of self-developed solutions go into production.
This paper examines the structural causes of this
discrepancy and shows why AI scaling is primarily an
architectural and governance decision—not purely a
technological one. The key is to treat AI as an enterprise
capability: with its own layers, maturity levels, and
governance mechanisms within the enterprise
architecture, with clear responsibilities for use cases, data,
and change, as well as for security and compliance.
Enterprise Architecture Management (EAM) provides the
integrative framework that connects strategy, technology,
organization, and compliance—provided that EAM itself
evolves and takes on the ongoing governance of this
landscape.
Understanding the problem: Four systematic anti-
patterns—Shadow AI, Pilot Purgatory, Governance
Vacuum, and Data Quality Deficits—prevent AI scaling in
companies. Thinking the architecture: A three-layer
architecture consisting of a Data & Knowledge Layer, an
AI Platform Layer, and an Application Layer forms the

Four anti-patterns systematically prevent AI scaling
and are mutually reinforcing. A three-layer architecture
provides the convergent organizing principle for scalable
Enterprise AI. Federated governance balances
decentralized innovation speed with central architectural
coherence. Organizational maturity and technical
ambition must grow in sync – stages cannot be skipped. A
dual-track strategy connects quick wins with sustainable
platform development from day one.
Generative AI has reached companies faster than any
technology wave before it. According to UBS estimates
based on web traffic data, ChatGPT reached around 100
million monthly active users in January 2023—about two
months after its release. The service was thus considered
the fastest-growing consumer application observed to
date; TikTok had taken about nine months to reach this
mark, while Instagram took two and a half years.[1] What
followed has affected nearly every industry. Menlo
Ventures estimates that US companies will invest around
$37 billion in generative AI in 2025—a good threefold
increase over the previous year.[2]
1

The returns are not keeping pace. According to a
particularly widely cited survey, around 95 percent of the
organizations studied report no measurable business value
from their AI initiatives.[3] The figure comes from the
MIT report The GenAI Divide and encapsulates a problem
that concerns CIOs and IT decision-makers worldwide:
the gap between potential and value creation is widening
instead of closing. Cross-industry analyses by McKinsey,
BCG, and ServiceNow paint the same picture.[4][5][6]
The 95 percent figure requires context. The MIT report
is a non-peer-reviewed snapshot and describes its own
findings as preliminary. It is based on 52 structured
interviews, 153 surveyed executives, and the analysis of
over 300 publicly documented AI initiatives. An
implementation is only considered successful if users or
executives report a significant and lasting impact.[3] We
therefore read the 95 percent as an order of magnitude, not
as a precise metric—and it suffices for the question at
hand.
Enterprise AI refers to the company-wide integration
of AI capabilities into business processes, products, and
services. Unlike isolated AI projects, the term stands for a
systematic, platform-based delivery of AI capabilities:
built once, usable by various business units and
applications.
This failure, according to the position of this paper, has
systematic causes that lie not only in the technology or a
lack of will. The bottleneck often lies in the fact that AI has
no architectural place in the corporate landscape—no
dedicated layer, no assigned responsibility, no place in the
target vision. This is exacerbated by unclear use case
prioritization, weak ownership, and a lack of change
capability, as well as by insufficient data maturity and
unresolved security and legal requirements. As long as
organizations treat AI as a collection of isolated tools and
pilot projects instead of as a strategic enterprise capability,
scaling, governance, and sustainable value creation will
fall by the wayside.
A. The Gap Between Investment and Value
Creation
In the same data, there is a large gap between
experimentation and productive operation. Around 80
percent of organizations have tested general AI assistants.
However, for self-developed tools tailored to their own
operations, the path narrows considerably: from
evaluation to pilot to productive use, only a fraction
remains—Chapter 4 details the stages.[3] Gartner
predicted as early as 2024 that at least 30 percent of
generative AI projects would be discontinued after the
proof of concept stage.[7] The findings published since
then suggest an even higher number.
This rate refers specifically to individually developed
enterprise AI solutions; for purchased specialized tools
and partnerships, the same report documents a
considerably higher success rate of around two-thirds.[3]
The choice of deployment model may therefore itself be
understood as an architectural decision.
For governance, this means: The path to scaling leads
less through individual development projects and more
through a platform strategy that systematically combines
external solutions and internal capabilities.

B. Why Technology is Not the Bottleneck
The widespread assumption that AI projects fail due to
technical limitations does not hold true. Model quality,
response times, and costs per request have improved
significantly since 2022.[8] The sticking points are usually
found elsewhere.
The MIT report cites a learning gap as the central cause.
[3] Current systems execute tasks but do not learn during
ongoing operations: They do not adapt to organization-
specific characteristics and do not improve over time.
Accordingly, the real challenges lie beyond the technology
—in project planning, in the collaboration between
business departments and IT, in data management, and in
the model lifecycle.[9] In all these areas, organizations
must first build capabilities, and this development requires
architectural foundations.
C. Four Anti-Patterns - One Common Cause
The causes of scaling failure can be grouped into four
patterns that occur across industries. Chapter 2 explores
them in detail; here is a preview.
Shadow AI describes the uncontrolled proliferation of
unauthorized AI tools. Employees resort to their own
solutions because central offerings are lacking or are
provided too slowly. This implies both: a loss of control
and an indication of what the official offering fails to
provide.
Pilot Purgatory is the scaling trap: Promising pilot
projects fail to make the transition to production. Between
a functioning prototype and a productive system lie data
connectivity, monitoring, and security. Moreover,
organizational anchoring is required.
The Governance Vacuum arises because AI adoption
outpaces the governance capabilities of organizations.
Existing IT governance structures do not address AI-
specific requirements.
Data Quality Deficits affect the foundation. Where
data management, quality, and responsibilities are not
established, even the most well-conceived AI architecture
will not hold up.
The four patterns rarely occur in isolation. Their
commonality is a void: AI has no defined place in the
enterprise architecture. Consequently, the answer to each
individual pattern also lies there.

D. The Architectural Perspective - A Thesis
The central thesis of this paper is as follows: The transition
from AI pilots to a scalable AI platform landscape is
2

CTI Research Paper Series • SSR
primarily an architectural and governance decision.
Technological maturity, talented teams, and ambitious
strategies are necessary but not sufficient conditions; the
same applies to suitable use cases, a viable ROI logic, and
the capacity for change. Organizations that treat AI scaling
exclusively as a technology decision will, with a high
probability, systematically falter.
A series of independent findings supports this view.
The literature on Enterprise AI Architecture describes a
well-conceived layering as the foundation for scaling.[10]
Across various reference architectures, the same three-
layer architecture is consistently repeated: a Data &
Knowledge Layer as the foundation, an AI Platform Layer
as the operational core, and an Application Layer as the
value creation layer.
On the governance side, it should be added that EAM is
the discipline with the broadest cross-functional mandate -
this paper accordingly derives its position that AI
governance belongs there.[11] Those who understand
EAM as a dynamic capability, with the dimensions of
Sensing, Seizing, and Transforming, have the framework
for the necessary further development.[12] Moreover, the
regulatory and normative framework—the EU AI Act as
applicable law, ISO/IEC 42001 and the NIST AI RMF as
voluntary standards—points in the same direction:
Anyone wishing to provide the expected evidence requires
an overview of the systems in use, a documented risk
assessment, traceable data provenance, and logged system
events. These are architectural artifacts.[13]
This architectural perspective is intentionally not to be
understood as monocausal. AI scaling equally depends on
use case selection, ROI logic, and change management. In
addition, there are ownership, operating model, and
vendor strategy, as well as qualifications and security and
legal capabilities. Architecture management does not
replace these factors; however, EAM forms the integrative
bracket within which they become visible, prioritizable,
and governable. Research on AI Governance and
organizational GenAI adoption confirms that successful
implementation requires an interplay of technological,
organizational, and environmental factors.[14][15]
E. What this paper provides - and what it does not
This paper is aimed at CIOs and IT decision-makers who
manage AI initiatives and are facing the question of how to
move from successful pilots to enterprise-wide scaling. It
is also addressed to Enterprise Architects who must
redefine their role for the AI era.
The focus is exclusively on the strategic governance
level: How does a company organize and govern its
overall AI landscape? This paper deliberately does not
provide an EU-AI-Act deep dive, a TOGAF tutorial, or a
vendor comparison of AI platforms.
The AI paradox - high investment with low scaling - is
not purely a technology problem, but primarily an
architecture and governance problem. The four central
anti-patterns can therefore not be eliminated individually;
they will recur as long as AI as an enterprise capability has
no place in the enterprise architecture. However, this
anchoring does not replace use case prioritization, change
management, and ownership, nor does it replace
qualification, security, and legal; it creates the framework
in which these factors can take effect in a coordinated

this gap, but must itself change to do so: from a blueprint

II. WHY AI INITIATIVES FAIL - A
PATTERN ANALYSIS
Four recurring anti-patterns - Shadow AI, Pilot
Purgatory, Governance Vacuum, and Data Quality
Deficits - explain a large part of the AI scaling problems in
companies. Each has structural causes that go beyond
technological limitations and demand architectural
answers. The four reinforce each other: each pattern
creates the conditions under which the next one grows -
and all four are rooted in the same architectural void.
Chapter 1 diagnosed the paradox: high AI investment
with low scaling success. Behind this are four anti-
patterns, which this chapter will address individually.
They are intertwined. A lack of governance promotes
Shadow AI, data quality deficits block the scaling of
pilots, and the resulting uncontrolled growth undermines
any subsequent governance.
Shadow AI refers to the unauthorized use of AI tools
and services by employees - without the knowledge,
approval, or control of the IT department. This refers to
two variants: the use of external AI services, such as
personal ChatGPT accounts for work tasks, and the
independent development of AI solutions outside of
official IT governance. The term is based on the
established concept of Shadow IT. However, the risk
profile is different: sensitive company data migrates into
external models, AI-supported decisions remain
untraceable, and model behavior can neither be centrally
controlled nor audited.
A. Shadow AI and Tool Sprawl - When Innovation
Overtakes Control
The most visible aspect is the sprawl. What begins as a
productivity gain for individual employees becomes a
systemic risk for data protection, compliance, and
architectural coherence within months.
In many cases, usage bypasses the IT department. In a
global survey of 48,340 people in 47 countries, 57 percent
of employees admitted to hiding their AI usage; nearly half
had entered company information into public AI tools.[1]
These are not occasional experiments. The Torii SaaS
Benchmark Report 2026 counts an average of 830
applications and a median of 680 in companies, attributing
61.3 percent of them to Shadow IT.[2] In micro-enterprises
with 11 to 50 employees, the density is highest: there are
269 unapproved AI tools per thousand employees.[3] The
consequences are familiar to every IT leader: duplicate
subscriptions, inconsistent data flows, and an AI landscape
that no one can fully oversee anymore.
The security implications are corresponding. The
Netskope Cloud and Threat Report 2026 records a
doubling of incidents compared to the previous year in
which employees submit sensitive data to AI applications:
an average of 223 such data policy violations per month
per organization. 47 percent of users access these via
personal, unmanaged accounts. This share has decreased
significantly from 78 percent in the previous year;
however, the number of incidents continues to rise.[4]
One-fifth of companies report data breaches directly
attributable to Shadow AI; these increase the cost of an
incident by an average of $670,000.[5] Moreover, security
4

RN Working Paper • August 2026
teams report longer identification times than for
conventional incidents.[5]
However, viewing Shadow AI solely as a risk is short-
sighted. The MIT report lists it as one of the most
important indicators of unmet business needs.[6] For the
broader phenomenon of Shadow IT, information systems
research has long since proven this dual nature: employees
circumvent official channels because they are too slow,
lack features, or approved alternatives are difficult to use.
[7] Thus, this circumvention can be interpreted. Every
unapproved use indicates which capability the
organization should offer centrally in the future. What is
new about the AI variant is solely the risk profile -
sensitive data in third-party models, opaque decision-
making processes, and probabilistic model behavior that is
difficult to control centrally.[8]
A prohibition does not appear to eliminate usage so
much as its visibility. Prohibited activity tends to migrate
to private devices and personal accounts, and thus beyond
the reach of any log that a security organization can
analyze. The consequence is that the risk overview may
deteriorate precisely at the point where compliance
statistics indicate success.
The architectural answer is therefore a central AI
platform that provides approved access to AI capabilities
and incorporates governance, data protection, and
compliance from the outset. If it is missing, employees
will fill the gap themselves - with all the consequences
described.
B. Pilot Purgatory - The Scaling Trap
Very few companies succeed in transitioning from a
successful pilot to regular operations. Several figures are
available on just how few, varying in reliability and
magnitude.
Pilot Purgatory describes the state in which
companies run numerous AI pilot projects that show
promising results in controlled environments but never
make the transition to productive, regular operations. The
pilots remain in a perpetual loop of extension, realignment,
and re-evaluation.
The MIT report GenAI Divide cites a figure of around
five percent for custom-developed AI tools that make it
into operation.[6] Chapter 1 noted the reservations about
the figures in this report—not peer-reviewed, small
sample size, strictly defined success criteria—and
therefore interpreted them as an order of magnitude. The
same applies here. Moreover, there is a point that is
relevant to the scaling issue: The count includes initiatives
that had already passed the pilot stage, not terminated
experiments. A vendor analysis estimates the share of
GenAI initiatives that fail to achieve their intended
outcome at 70 to 85 percent; this range is an extrapolation
from older data, not a new survey.[9] The orders of
magnitude differ, but the direction is consistent.
The causes extend far beyond technology. The MIT
report first cites the learning gap: Today's systems do not
learn from business reality, do not adapt to changing
requirements on their own, and do not improve over time.
[6] This is hardly noticeable in the pilot phase. Intensive
support, curated datasets, and dedicated experts
compensate for this deficiency. In regular operations, with
4

CTI Research Paper Series • SSR
real data volumes and scarce resources, this model is no
longer sustainable.
Moreover, time is a factor. What a medium-sized
company accomplishes in about three months often takes
nine months or longer in a large corporation—these are
empirical values from transformation projects, not
collected metrics. Within this window, priorities shift,
budget cycles expire, and sponsors change roles. New
topics displace the original initiative. Ultimately, it dies
due to the organization's attention span, not a technical
problem.
Companies that develop AI solutions with specialized
partners achieve production readiness about twice as often
as those that rely solely on their own resources.[6] Thus,
Pilot Purgatory is also a competence problem.
Architectural design, MLOps, and governance
implementation are different skills from those required for
a successful pilot. Research on collaboration problems in
ML projects supports this: The transition to production
requires an orderly interaction between Data Scientists,
ML Engineers, and Operations, which is rarely established
in pilot projects.[11]
The pattern is the same everywhere. Pilots run in a
protected environment that cannot be transferred to
operations: without connection to the central data
infrastructure, without monitoring, without governance
processes. The architectural answer, therefore, is a scaling
architecture that is considered from the very beginning.
C. The Governance Vacuum - When Control
Cannot Keep Pace with Speed
A gap is widening between the pace of AI adoption and the
ability to govern it. Unlike Shadow AI and Pilot Purgatory,
its effects are not immediately operational. Its risks are
strategic and regulatory in nature and often only manifest
with a delay.
Only 40 percent of employees state that their workplace
has any policy or guidance on the use of generative AI.[1]
Meanwhile, a large majority of executives report that the
pace of adoption exceeds their organization's governance
capabilities.[12] This is less an oversight than a mismatch
of cycle times. AI tools are introduced in minutes;
governance structures take months.
This vacuum becomes visible on three levels.
Strategically, many organizations lack the assignment of
responsibility. Who approves the use of a new LLM-based
service? Who classifies the risk? Who is liable if the model
makes a wrong decision? Without answers, AI initiatives
remain in a no-man's-land between IT, business
departments, and compliance.[13]
Operationally, the mechanisms that make governance
possible in the first place are missing. Without an AI
system registry, no one reliably knows which models are
in use, what data they process, and into which risk
category they fall.[14] Without defined review processes,
individual teams unilaterally decide on probabilistic
models in business-critical processes. And without
monitoring, Model Drift, bias, and declining performance
remain undetected until damage occurs.
Regulatorily, the pressure is mounting. The EU AI Act
requires evidence for high-risk systems that cannot be
reconstructed from documents after the fact: risk
classification and technical documentation, automatic

deadlines for this were pushed back to July 2026; Chapter
5 details the current status. Those without governance
structures in place must still build all of this, and the
gained lead time is shorter than it appears.
From this three-tiered vacuum emerges an insight that
underpins the rest of this paper: Governance without
architecture remains a documented declaration of intent.
Policies that are not translated into technical guardrails and

D. Data Quality Deficits - The Underestimated
Foundation
The data foundation is discussed the least, as model
architecture and Agentic AI capture the attention.
However, it underpins everything else. Without robust
data, every AI initiative is built on a weak foundation,
regardless of model quality and ambition.
Gartner quantifies it: 63 percent of organizations do not
have suitable data management practices for AI—or do not
know if they do.[16] The same analysis predicts that by
2026, organizations will discontinue approximately 60
percent of those AI projects that are not based on an AI-
ready data foundation.[16] The literature aligns with this.
Data quality regularly appears there as the most common
technical reason for failure.[17]
Three dimensions are intertwined here. The first is data
quality: Incomplete, contradictory, and outdated data leads
to models that systematically miss the mark in operation.
This is one of the reasons for Pilot Purgatory—curated test
data and real-world corporate data are two different
worlds. The second is availability. Many organizations
have enough data but cannot access it: silos between
business units, incompatible formats, unresolved access
rights. The third is data governance—which data may be
used for which model and how its origin and processing
can be traced. All three require a data lineage
infrastructure that makes the path of the data from source
to model output visible.[18]

Data maturity can hardly be introduced retroactively
into an ongoing AI initiative. The evidence suggests that it
should be established for the respective use case before the
model and the platform are selected. This is not an
argument for spending years constructing an enterprise-
wide data foundation before the first pilot begins; what is
at issue is the sequence in each individual case—first the
data foundation that the specific use case requires, then the
technology built upon it. Organizations that reverse this
sequence tend to create structural instabilities that
intensify with each stage of scaling.
E. The Common Deficit - A Lack of Architectural
Anchoring
At first glance, the four patterns have little in common.
Shadow AI concerns user behavior, Pilot Purgatory the
scaling capability, the governance vacuum the control
structures, and data quality deficits the technical
foundation. However, they share a common cause: in none
of the four cases is AI a subject of the enterprise
architecture—it happens alongside it.
Therefore, the patterns feed each other. If an approved
platform is missing, employees resort to their own tools—
and each of these stores data in places that no registry
knows. The governance vacuum thus grows with the
uncontrolled proliferation it has allowed. Conversely,
weak data quality keeps pilots confined to the protected
environment in which they function; the longer one
remains there, the more attractive a quick, home-grown
solution from the business department becomes. For
governance, this means that individual measures are
ineffective. A ban without a platform offering merely
shifts the problem, and a guideline without an AI system
registry governs a landscape that no one sees. Companies
that succeed in scaling differ from those stuck in the pilot
stage primarily in that they understand AI as a strategic
platform capability.[19] Research on AI readiness factors
points in the same direction: successful adoption requires
maturity in strategy, data, and technology, as well as in
organization and governance.[10]
The architectural answer to all four is developed in the
following chapter.
For governance, this leads to an uncomfortable
consequence: there is no sequence in which the four
patterns can be tackled one after another. Because each
creates the conditions for the next, an individual measure
has at best a local and usually only a short-term effect. The
four architectural answers—a central platform, a scaling
architecture, EA-integrated governance, and a Data
Foundation—only interlock when they are conceived as a
single structure. This shifts the initial question: it is no
longer which of the four problems to solve first, but rather
what overall order the AI landscape should have.

III. AI AS AN ENTERPRISE
CAPABILITY - THE ARCHITECTURAL
PERSPECTIVE
Project-based thinking about AI structurally creates
fragmentation—the shift to platform thinking is an
architectural necessity, not a technological option. Across
all reference architectures, a three-layer architecture
consisting of a Data & Knowledge Layer, an AI Platform
Layer, and an Application Layer is converging as a cross-
industry organizing principle. The choice between a
central, federated, and hybrid architecture depends on the
context; regardless, six architectural principles support
scaling—including Security by Design, which becomes a
cross-functional aspect with productive access to
corporate data.
What the four anti-patterns from Chapter 2 lack can
also be formulated positively: an order in which AI
systems, data, and models have a defined place, and rules
that apply to all initiatives. Both are matters of design
work. It begins with a decision that precedes any
technology choice—whether to treat AI as a project or as a
platform.
A. From Projects to Platforms - The Paradigm
Shift
No company would let each department build its own
power grid. With AI, however, this is precisely the norm:
The vast majority of companies still treat it as a collection
of isolated projects. A business unit identifies a use case, a
team builds a prototype, a pilot delivers promising results
—and then the initiative stagnates. This pattern repeats
across the entire organization, resulting in a patchwork of
point solutions that are neither compatible with each other
nor utilize a common infrastructure. Research on AI
scaling attributes this fragmentation to a project-based
mindset:[1] Each project builds its own data pipelines,
selects its own models, and defines its own quality
standards. The result is data silos and redundant
infrastructure; the maintenance effort grows with every
new initiative.
A study by Carnegie Mellon University investigated
the specific obstacles in transitioning from ML
experiments to production. It identified communication
deficits, insufficient documentation, and fragmented
engineering processes as the central causes for the failure
of ML projects.[2] These findings extend far beyond
technical limitations: Different professional jargon
between data scientists and software engineers, a lack of
artifact standardization, and unclear responsibilities for
production operations create friction that is repeated with
every isolated project.
The Enterprise AI Capability is the organization-
wide ability to deploy AI systematically and repeatably.
Chapter 1 described Enterprise AI as a state: AI is
integrated into business processes, products, and services
across the company. The capability defines what sustains
this state. It is measurable by what multiple initiatives
share: infrastructure, standardized processes, and reusable
components. This is complemented by a governance
framework that applies to all. Thus, Enterprise AI is the
goal, while the capability is the prerequisite to achieve and
maintain it.
7

RN Working Paper • August 2026
The path from projects to platforms has been
demonstrated by cloud migration. Initially, companies
moved individual applications to the cloud, project by
project and without an overarching architecture. What
emerged was uncontrolled hybrid growth. It only became
manageable through platform strategies centered on the
Landing Zone: an architectural pattern that defines the
common infrastructure for all workloads—network
segmentation, central Identity and Access Management, a
unified logging infrastructure, and defined compliance
baselines. Once established, it applied to every application
that followed. The same holds true for AI: The paradigm
shift introduces AI as a platform capability;[3] without an
analogous platform strategy, the AI transformation will
remain a stream of isolated individual projects with no
cumulative added value.
With platform thinking, the organizational logic shifts.
Investments are then directed toward a common
foundation with reusable components: central data
pipelines, standardized model services, and uniform
governance mechanisms, supplemented by shared
monitoring tools.[1] Business units consume these
services; custom building is no longer necessary.
Empirical research supports this: Companies that
successfully scale AI differ from stagnating organizations
in their ability to conceptualize AI as a strategic platform
capability.[4]
Investment figures show how rapidly the field is
growing. In 2025, companies are estimated to invest $37
billion in generative AI—an increase by a factor of 3.2
compared to the previous year.[5] Of this, $19 billion
flowed into the application layer and $18 billion into the
infrastructure layer.[5] Consequently, the market has long
been organized in layers. The open question is whether
companies will replicate this structure internally or
continue to make scattered purchases.
The difference is most evident in the budget. A project
is justified by its individual business case, whereas a
platform is justified by the cumulative value of all use
cases running on it. This transition, therefore, reaches deep
into the organization: funding logic, governance
structures, and responsibilities change accordingly.[1]
This shift in argumentation is one of the biggest hurdles on
the path to scaling; Chapter 6 explores it in greater detail.
The most frequently observed error in the platform
transition is that organizations build the platform without a
concrete use-case pull. Capability that no one demands
generates cost without generating value. Organizations
that succeed tend to begin with individual use cases and
allow the platform to grow around them.
B. The Three-Layer Architecture as an
Organizing Principle
Providers name their building blocks differently. When
comparing the reference architectures for Enterprise AI—
the offerings of cloud providers, the frameworks of
analysts, the academic models—the same structure is
almost always found underneath: Despite differing
terminology and levels of detail, nearly all approaches
organize the field into three layers.[6] We will henceforth
refer to this organizational model as the Three-Layer
Architecture. The reason for this is structural. Only when
data management, model operations, and application
7

CTI Research Paper Series • SSR
integration are decoupled from one another can a system
be scaled, maintained, and governed.
Sculley et al. described early on what happens when
this separation is missing: ML systems without clear layer
separation accumulate significant technical debt.[7] The
actual ML code typically constitutes only a fraction of a
production system. The vast majority is dedicated to data
collection, feature extraction, and serving infrastructure,
as well as configuration management and monitoring. If
these components are not systematically separated and
managed, hidden dependencies arise that make any change
to the system an incalculable risk. Google demonstrated
how this separation can be implemented in a production
environment with TFX, a platform for ML pipelines: data
validation, model training, and serving are explicitly
separated.[8] TFX established uniform building blocks for
each step of the pipeline and defined clear interfaces
between them; consequently, individual parts could be
further developed and exchanged independently of one
another.
The Data & Knowledge Layer supports everything
that lies above it. It brings together the components for the
collection and storage, integration, and provision of data,
in addition to knowledge representations.[9] At its core,
this includes Data Lakes and Data Warehouses as central
storage systems, Feature Stores for the consistent delivery
of ML features, and vector databases for semantic search
and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)—that is, for
answers that a language model composes from specifically
retrieved company documents; Knowledge Graphs map
structured relationships. Upwards, the layer delivers what
the others need: reliable, consistent, and accessible data.
This is precisely where the bottleneck lies: 63 percent of
organizations do not have suitable data management
practices for AI—or do not know if they do.[10]

As an organizational framework, the Three-Layer

destabilize the business applications in the Application

Data & Knowledge Layer should automatically benefit all
services above it.

MLOps (Machine Learning Operations) transfers the

The discipline encompasses practices, technologies, and a

performance monitoring.

probabilistic model outputs into business-relevant

business applications, Copilot integrations, and
autonomous agents. The economic focus is also here: Of
the 19 billion dollars that flowed into the application layer
of generative AI in 2025, the largest share among domain-
specific applications, at 4 billion dollars, was allocated to
coding assistants.[5]

C. A Comparison of Architectural Patterns
This, however, does not yet decide who operates what. The
three layers define which functions an enterprise AI
architecture must cover; how these functions are
distributed organizationally and technically is determined
by the choice between three architectural patterns: central,
federated, or hybrid.[6]
The central AI platform consolidates all AI activities
on a unified infrastructure. A dedicated AI Center of
Excellence operates the platform and provides
8

standardized services for the entire organization.[1] This
leads to efficient resource utilization, uniform standards,
and simplified governance. The downside becomes
apparent with growing demand: the central team becomes
a bottleneck, domain-specific requirements are not
adequately met, and the pace of innovation in business
departments suffers from waiting times. This pattern is
particularly suitable for organizations with a manageable
AI portfolio and high regulatory pressure.
The federated approach distributes AI capabilities
across decentralized teams and domains that develop and
operate models autonomously. This is primarily a matter
of responsibility: decentralized teams assume full
responsibility for their data products and models—
including quality assurance and governance. This
autonomy fosters domain-specific expertise; however, it
requires explicit agreements on interoperability standards

Which pattern is suitable depends on the context: the

D. Architectural Principles for Scalable Enterprise
AI
The organizational model varies, but the underlying
principles should not. Five are considered prerequisites for
scalable Enterprise AI, regardless of whether an
organization builds in a centralized, federated, or hybrid
manner; each can be translated into concrete design
decisions.
If a new language model cripples the business
application, Separation of Concerns is missing: the
consistent decoupling of data, models, and applications.
[11] It is directly reflected in the three-layer architecture.
Changes in data management should not force changes to
the models, and a model change should not require an
adaptation of the business application. In practice, this
means: clearly defined interfaces between the layers, no
direct dependencies. The violation of this principle is one
of the most common reasons why pilot projects fail to
scale: they merge data preparation, model logic, and
application integration into a monolithic system that can
only be changed as a whole. Sculley et al. have described
9

therefore often found in organizations with strict data
protection requirements or highly heterogeneous business
areas.
The hybrid model combines elements of both
approaches and has established itself in practice as the
most common compromise.[6] A common pattern is the
hub-and-spoke model: a central hub provides common
infrastructure, standards, and governance, while spokes in
the business departments are responsible for domain-
specific data and applications. The interfaces between the
hub and spokes follow clearly defined APIs and
governance rules. This pattern is oriented towards the data
mesh concept, which combines decentralized data
ownership with central interoperability and federated
governance. Platform literature describes this dynamic as
a balancing act between central control and local
autonomy.[3] The trade-off never disappears; the
architecture must make it manageable.

API-First requires that all AI services are accessible
via defined, standardized interfaces.[11] No model, no
Feature Store, and no monitoring dashboard exists without
an API. Thus, components become reusable, integration
into existing IT systems becomes simpler, and business
departments can engage in self-service. In the context of
generative AI, this principle gains additional weight: LLM
gateways, which control access to various language
models via a unified API, allow for flexible switching
between providers and reduce vendor lock-in.
Data Lineage by Design ensures that the origin and
processing of data remain traceable at all times.[9] For AI
systems, this principle is critical for two reasons: First, the
quality and composition of the training data directly
influence model behavior—without lineage, incorrect
behavior cannot be diagnosed. Second, regulatory
frameworks such as the EU AI Act require explicit
documentation of data origin for high-risk AI systems.
Modern data platforms support automated lineage tracking
across all processing stages—from the raw source through
Feature Engineering to the model decision. Without
lineage, both are missing: diagnostics during operation
and proof for regulatory authorities.
AI systems differ from deterministic software through
phenomena such as Concept Drift and Data Drift: Model
performance can decline over time without a technical
error being present. Observability is aimed precisely at
this—going beyond classic monitoring to the continuous
9

CTI Research Paper Series • SSR
measurability of model behavior, costs, and performance.
[11] An observability infrastructure detects degradation
early and initiates retraining or manual reviews. It also
creates cost transparency per use case, a key prerequisite
for the business management of the AI portfolio.
Governance as Code embeds policies directly into the
platform. The model is Infrastructure as Code, the DevOps
principle of machine-readable infrastructure definition;
applied to AI platforms, it means enforcing governance
policies automatically.[13] Compliance checks run in the
pipeline, model deployments pass through defined quality
gates, and risk classifications automatically determine the
required review process. MLOps research shows what this
looks like: Pipelines integrate validation steps for data
quality, model performance, and fairness metrics before a
deployment is approved.[11] Thus, the principle addresses
the governance vacuum from Chapter 2. The fact that only
a minority of employees are even aware of a policy on AI
use is often because governance is designed as a separate,
manual process. As code, compliance is integrated into the
development and deployment process.
These five principles are intentionally formulated to be
framework-agnostic. They apply regardless of whether an
organization works with TOGAF, Zachman, or its own
enterprise architecture (EA) framework, and are also
independent of the chosen cloud strategy. Their
implementation follows a maturity logic: A company at
the beginning of its AI integration will first implement
API-First for its most important model services before
building a complete service mesh architecture—a
consistently managed traffic layer for the exchange of all
services with each other. A sixth principle is added as soon
as AI works with enterprise data in production; it is the
subject of the following section.
E. Security Architecture as a Cross-Cutting
Function
Once AI is operating productively, the attack and risk
surface expands compared to traditional application
architecture. The systems access large, often sensitive
datasets, use external model and cloud services, and
generate decisions that must remain traceable and
verifiable. Security is therefore a cross-cutting function
and must be embedded in all three layers.[13]
Data classification is the starting point. The Data &
Knowledge Layer requires role- and attribute-based access
control, encryption, and Data Loss Prevention against
unintentional data leakage; moreover, the traceability of
data provenance and usage is essential. RAG and LLM
scenarios in particular increase the demands on
authorization concepts: Retrieval components must
prevent users from accessing information via semantic
search to which they are not entitled in the source systems.
[9]
One layer higher, the AI Platform Layer centralizes
control: Model access, Secrets Management, and gateway
architectures are managed centrally here, as are prompt
and output filters, Guardrails, and monitoring and audit
logs. An LLM gateway can do more than just manage
costs and vendor access: It can enforce policies—such as
permitted models, data leakage controls, and logging, as
well as Rate Limits and approval processes. Thus, Security
by Design becomes part of the platform architecture and
1

RN Working Paper • August 2026
does not remain the responsibility of each individual
application.[11]
At the Application Layer, the question shifts from
access to accountability. It requires role-based usage,
secure integration into business processes, and human-in-
the-loop mechanisms—meaning mandatory review by a
human—and clear accountability for every AI-supported
decision. Architecturally, it follows that Security Patterns,
predefined control points, and Zero-Trust principles—i.e.,
no implicit trust in networks or systems—must be as
mandatory for AI platforms as API and MLOps standards.
[14] Those who omit these controls ultimately scale the
risk surface along with their operations.
Thus, Security by Design joins the five from section
3.4 as the sixth principle. It shares their character: a design
decision that is reflected in interfaces, pipelines, and
control points. How organizations determine their current
maturity level and define the next realistic step is the
subject of the following chapter.
The three layers are less a target state and more a
decision-making framework: Every single AI decision—a
model change, a new data source, a purchased assistant—
can be positioned within it, and its consequences for the
other layers become assessable. Its practical value lies in
its durability. Vendors, models, and tools change at short
intervals; the separation of data management, model
operation, and application outlasts these cycles, and so do
the six principles from Separation of Concerns to Security
by Design. An organization that has mandated them once
does not need to re-address the architecture question with
the next technology wave.

IV. MATURITY LEVELS AND
EVOLUTIONARY PATHS
Established maturity models converge on common
dimensions: Strategy, data, and technology, as well as
organization and governance, determine an
organization's AI maturity level. This paper adds the
maturity of Enterprise Architecture Management (EAM)
as a sixth dimension. Technical evolutionary stages, from
Basic Prompting to multi-agent systems, each require
specific architectural prerequisites that cannot be
improvised. Technical ambition without organizational
and architectural maturity creates architectural debt—
stages cannot be skipped; however, the throughput speed
can be influenced.
A target architecture is of little use as long as the
starting point is undefined. Anyone planning a platform
without knowing which models are already running
productively in-house and who is accountable for them is
planning in a vacuum. Maturity models provide this
positioning: They translate architectural complexity into
an ordered sequence of stages, which can guide investment
decisions as well as the development of governance and
competence. Chapter 3 described where the path leads;
this chapter is about where it begins.

For the perspective of this paper, a sixth dimension is
added: the maturity of Enterprise Architecture
Management. It can hardly be separated from the AI
maturity level. Where architectural principles are lacking
and target architectures remain non-binding,[1][8] AI
pilots certainly emerge—however, in our observation,
they are rarely scaled. Conversely, higher EAM maturity
lays the foundation for central platform services, for
reusable components, and for traceable data origin (data
lineage), as well as for machine-enforced rules
(Governance as Code) and portfolio-wide prioritization.
This leads to the hypothesis of this paper: The maturity of
architecture management moderates the probability of
success for AI implementations because it determines
whether use cases can evolve from isolated experiments
into productive, integrated capabilities. It is a well-
founded assumption, not a finding—its verification is still
pending.
In the consulting practice of the authors, self-
assessments regularly prove to be overly optimistic; this
observation has not been formally surveyed, but it recurs
consistently. Organizations assessing themselves tend to
count what is visible—tools and licenses, successful
demonstrations—while what is absent goes unnoticed in
the count. Functioning ChatGPT access and three pilot
projects do not constitute stage 3. They typically
correspond to stage 1: the technological equipment is in
place, whereas the organizational and procedural
prerequisites of higher maturity levels are not.
B. Technical Stages of Evolution of AI Integration
Alongside the organizational maturity level, a second
development is taking place: the way in which AI is
technically integrated into systems and processes. With
each stage, the number of integration points and the
demand for control increase, and each stage requires
certain architectural foundations. Where a license
agreement is sufficient at the beginning, permissions,
logging, and intervention rights are ultimately tied to each
individual processing step. If these foundations are
missing, the transition to the next stage will be unstable or
will fail.
A separate framework exists for the technical side. In
Nature Communications, Lavin et al. have presented a
model of Technology Readiness Levels (TRL) for
machine learning: a scale for technical maturity that allows
ML systems to be systematically classified.[9] Their ten
stages range from Level 0 to 9; Level 0 is the innovation
compared to the classic TRL model. Their logic aligns
with the one presented here: progress in maturity depends
on preliminary work that cannot be retroactively
completed during the ongoing project.
1

RN Working Paper • August 2026
Stage 1 - Basic Prompting is the entry point. Someone
opens a browser tab, describes their task, and manually
transfers the result to the system where the work is actually
performed. Employees use generative AI tools via their
standard interfaces, without connection to internal systems
and without central control. Architecturally, this costs
almost nothing: internet access and a license budget are
sufficient. Accordingly, the business value remains
selective and individual—it can be neither measured nor
scaled.[10]
With Stage 2 - Copilot Integration, the systematic
embedding begins. AI assistants work within existing
applications, for example, as Microsoft 365 Copilot,
GitHub Copilot, or as an AI function in customer
management (Customer Relationship Management, CRM)
and in resource planning (Enterprise Resource Planning,
ERP). This significantly increases the requirements:
Identity Management must accommodate AI usage, data
permissions must apply down to the document level, and
initial governance policies are needed. The value lies in
productivity gains—limited by the respective application
platform.[5]
Stage 3 - Tool Augmentation goes beyond built-in
copilot capabilities and extends existing systems with
proprietary AI functions: text analysis in ticketing
systems, automated document classification, AI-supported
anomaly detection. Here, the three-layer architecture
described in Chapter 3 becomes a prerequisite, at least in
its basic form: the Data & Knowledge Layer for data
access, the AI Platform Layer for API connectivity and
Model Serving (the controlled deployment of models),
plus defined interfaces to the Application Layer, where the
business applications reside.[11]
At Stage 4 - Engine Approaches, AI becomes an
independent service with standardized APIs. AI models
run as independent services that various business
applications consume: a Recommendation Engine serves
the webshop, the mobile app, and the call center system
equally. This requires a fully operationalized AI Platform
Layer—with a model directory (Model Registry) and
automated MLOps pipelines, with versioning and
monitoring.[11] Feature Stores keep the input data
consistent for all consumers, and an API gateway manages
access and versioning. If this infrastructure is lacking, the
integration effort is repeated for each new consumer.
Currently, the upper end is formed by Stage 5 - Multi-
Agent and LLM-native Systems. An agent receives a
complaint, reconciles delivery and inventory data from
two separate systems, initiates the replacement delivery,
and only presents the disputed case to a human.
Autonomous AI agents take on complex, multi-stage
tasks, orchestrate each other, and make decisions
themselves within defined limits. Business processes here
are AI-native: the AI drives the process instead of
supplementing it. Architecturally, this demands an
orchestration layer for agent communication, robust
guardrails for what an agent is allowed to do, and real-time
monitoring from which interventions can be made—and
above that, a governance structure that clearly assigns
responsibilities for machine-based decisions.
Two forecasts from Gartner illustrate how quickly this
stage will achieve widespread adoption—and how often it
will fail. By 2028, around 33 percent of enterprise
12

CTI Research Paper Series • SSR
software is expected to contain Agentic AI, meaning
agents capable of action rather than supportive assistants,
compared to less than one percent in 2024. At the same
time, Gartner expects that over 40 percent of Agentic AI
projects will be discontinued by the end of 2027; the
analysis cites escalating costs, unclear business value, and
insufficient risk controls as reasons.[12] None of these
three reasons lies in the model quality.
Agentic AI refers to AI systems that independently
plan and execute complex, multi-stage tasks, interacting
with other systems and agents, and making decisions
autonomously within defined limits. A copilot waits for
human instruction; an agent acts on its own initiative.
Accordingly, the requirements for governance are higher.
C. The Correlation - Organizational Maturity ×
Level of Technical Evolution
Stage 4 technology can be bought; however, it cannot be
reliably operated at maturity level 2. Both axes are
interconnected, and this connection can be broken by
neither a budget increase nor a change in technology.
Those who aim for a multi-agent architecture at maturity
level 2 rarely fail because of the technology—they lack
governance, data quality, and the appropriate
organizational and competency structures.
This interpretation is not proven; however, it is
supported from two sides. The finding reported in Chapter
2—that only five percent reach production—can plausibly
be attributed to the maturity level of the organizations
involved; this correlation, however, was not measured
there. Jöhnk et al. show that organizations that ignore
individual readiness dimensions fail regardless of the
quality of their technical implementation.[1] Lavin et al.
describe the same from a technical perspective: ML
systems at low TRL levels have fundamentally different
requirements for infrastructure, data quality, and
governance than systems at high levels. Skipping this
requirement profile introduces systemic instabilities.[9]
The most common anti-pattern has a name: technically
ambitious, organizationally immature. Its course is
predictable. A business department builds the engine, the
platform is created afterward, and data responsibility
remains unresolved. In the end, there is infrastructure, but
no one systematically monitors model quality, the results
fluctuate with the data situation, and no single entity is
responsible for wrong decisions. What remains is
technical debt that grows with every additional use case. It
is the Hidden Technical Debt described in Chapter 3 with
Sculley et al.: The effort lies in the periphery of the model,
and that is also where the debt grows.[13]

This leads to an encouraging conclusion for management:
Those who adhere to the sequence are not condemned to

architectural foundation for each subsequent step, rather
than aiming for the highest stage as quickly as possible.
Moreover, the pace can be increased with external
experience. The MIT survey reports that companies
collaborating with external partners on AI scaling are
about twice as likely to achieve production deployment as

Those who have already supported a scaling process know

D. Diagnostics - Where Does My Company Stand?
A useful self-assessment requires questions that cannot be
answered by good will alone. What is insightful here is not
so much the target state, but rather the dimension that is
currently holding back the next step. The following
guiding questions lead through such an assessment along
the six dimensions introduced.
Strategy: Is AI found as a strategic priority in the
board's planning documents—with concrete goals and
metrics, or merely as a declaration of intent? Are use cases
prioritized by business value and are investments managed
centrally?
Data: Are the relevant data sources inventoried and
accessible via standardized interfaces? Are there defined
quality metrics and automated validation mechanisms?
Are responsibilities for data ownership and data lineage
clearly assigned? Chapter 2 has quantified how rarely
these prerequisites are met.[14]
Technology: Does the organization operate AI models
in a standardized environment with versioning,
monitoring, and automated deployment pipelines—or are
development and deployment carried out individually for
each project? Is there a central model registry? How many
productive AI applications consume central platform
services—and how many operate parallel proprietary
infrastructure?[11]
13

CTI Research Paper Series • SSR
Organization and Culture: Are there dedicated roles
such as the Enterprise AI Architect? Are there cross-
functional teams of data scientists, ML engineers, and
subject matter experts? Is there a Center of Excellence,
i.e., a permanent body that sets standards and disseminates
knowledge? Research on collaboration challenges shows
that the transition from experiment to production requires
specific organizational capabilities that go beyond purely
technical competencies.[15]
Governance: Is the organization aware of the entirety
of its productive AI applications? Is there an AI system
register? Are risk classification and responsibilities
documented? Are there operational governance
mechanisms—such as architecture reviews—or is
governance limited to policy documents without
operational enforcement?
EAM Maturity: Are there binding architectural
principles, current architecture and data inventories,
established review processes, and a target state that guides
concrete AI initiatives? Are architectural decisions
documented and tracked—or does EAM primarily remain
a retrospective documentation function? Aligning with
established architecture maturity models helps to
systematically assess this question—for instance, with the
Architecture Capability Maturity logic of The Open Group
Architecture Framework (TOGAF), which is widely used
in enterprise architecture.[8]
The dimensions are rarely developed evenly. An
organization can operate at level 3 technologically while
remaining at level 1 in governance and data. The platform
then bears loads for which its rules were never designed.
These asymmetries give rise to architectural debt.
Consequently, the most important principle is: The next
step carries more weight than the final goal. A company at
level 2 does not need a detailed plan for level 5. It needs a
robust plan for the transition to level 3.
The question remains as to who is responsible for this
plan. A diagnosis does not steer anything by itself; it
requires a discipline that authoritatively sets the next step,
monitors its adherence, and keeps the existing landscape in
view—the subject of the following chapter.
According to the models referenced here,
organizational maturity and the technical stage of
evolution are closely linked; a study that measures this fit
itself is not available. At the governance level, the
connection becomes tangible: Without binding
architectural principles, current inventories, and effective
review mechanisms, the institutional foundation for
scaling is missing. For practice, a rule of priority follows
from this. The pace of an AI transformation is determined
by the weakest of the six dimensions—an organization
with an excellent platform and unassigned data
responsibility will only get as far as its data responsibility
allows. Therefore, investments belong where the diagnosis
reveals the largest gap, even if this is less visible than the
next model change.

V. ENTERPRISE ARCHITECTURE AS A
GOVERNANCE INSTRUMENT FOR AI
AI systems and models belong in the EA metamodel as
independent building blocks, along with data lineage and
the risk category for each system; together with the
appropriate governance mechanisms, EAM becomes a
governance instrument for AI. Federated governance
14

CTI Research Paper Series • SSR
resolves the innovation dilemma between decentralized
experimentation speed and central architectural
coherence. Regulation codifies what is architecturally
sensible anyway: The required evidence is generated in
the system landscape, not in the compliance department—
and the deadlines for high-risk systems, postponed in July
2026, provide time for development, not a deferral.
Who steers the path from AI pilots to a scalable
platform? This paper takes the position that Enterprise
Architecture Management, due to its connecting function,
is the natural home for this task—however, only if EAM
itself evolves. And that begins with an uncomfortable
finding: EA does not enjoy a good reputation in many
organizations.
A. The EA Renaissance - From a Documentation
Tool to a Steering Instrument
What do business departments think of when they hear
"Enterprise Architecture"? Usually, a colleague who
maintains diagrams that no one ever looks at, and a
committee that pushes projects back by a quarter. This
reputation has a history. Traditional EA frameworks like
TOGAF (The Open Group Architecture Framework) and
Zachman emerged in an era when IT landscapes were
relatively stable, and architectural decisions could be
planned quarters or even years in advance.[1] The result
was thick blueprints that were already outdated by the time
they were completed. Anyone looking to manage an AI
portfolio today is confronted with precisely this legacy.
Enterprise Architecture Management (EAM): EAM
refers to the systematic design and governance of a
company's overall architecture—from business
capabilities, data, and applications to the technological
infrastructure. The goal is to align every architectural
decision with the company's objectives.
Almost everything about this description is correct. It
reflects the practice of many EA functions, but not the
potential of the discipline: The weakness lay in the
execution. Operating EA purely as a documentation tool is
like using a navigation system only to read maps without
activating the route guidance. Ross, Weill, and Robertson
pointed this out as early as 2006: The enterprise
architecture must form the foundation for business
execution and not end as a downstream documentation
exercise.[2] A meta-analysis on the contribution of
Enterprise Architecture to the success of digital
transformation primarily reports two effects: The
complexity of digital ecosystems becomes manageable,
and strategic alignment increases.[1]
With digital transformation, the requirements for
Enterprise Architecture have shifted. Verhoef et al. divide
the path into three stages—Digitization, Digitalization,
and Digital Transformation—each requiring different
architectural governance capabilities.[3] AI marks the
transition to the third stage, in which business models
themselves must be architecturally re-envisioned. Those
who merely digitize existing processes will not get there.
AI-native business logics are emerging that must be
designed from the ground up.
An internal assistant that searches documents is rarely
just an application: It accesses document repositories,
depends on permissions, model access, and logging, and
raises the question of who is functionally responsible for
1

RN Working Paper • August 2026
the information it provides. Thus, AI forces the cross-
connections that EA was always meant to establish—
across data strategy and the application landscape, across
technology infrastructure, compliance, and business
processes. No other governance discipline connects these
dimensions with the same breadth.[4] Bharadwaj et al.
argued as early as 2013 that digital strategy and corporate
strategy must merge—a thesis that is confirmed in a more
acute form by AI.[5] EAM is the operational instrument
for this merger because it brings together business
capabilities and data flows with governance requirements
in a coherent model, including technology components.
Conceptually, this connection is called Dynamic
Capability—EA as the ability to recognize technological
changes (Sensing), develop architectural responses
(Seizing), and adapt the organization and architecture
accordingly (Transforming).[4] This is precisely what the
introduction of AI demands from organizations. A
methodological limitation must be mentioned here: The
specific causal relationship between the maturity of
Enterprise Architecture Management and the success of
AI scaling has not yet been validated by dedicated
empirical studies. The argument is based on converging
evidence from EA research, AI adoption research, and
practical application. Direct proof remains an important
desideratum for future studies.
Enterprise Architecture can assume the role of a
governance body only if it transforms itself. Attempts to
govern AI with the instruments of EA 1.0—static models,
quarterly reviews, a documentation-centric approach—
risk producing precisely the bureaucracy that drives AI
teams toward Shadow AI, that is, toward tools procured
and paid for without the involvement of the IT function.
Three levers turn the documentation tool into a steering
instrument. The first lies in the review process: away from
the quarterly approval gate and toward continuous,
lightweight guidance. Moreover, the artifacts are changing
—from static documents to living, data-driven models.
And the role itself is shifting: The blueprinter is becoming
a strategic advisor who sets guardrails and identifies
decision options.[4]
B. AI as a First-Class Citizen in the EA
Metamodel
What is not represented in the metamodel can hardly be
governed later. Traditional metamodels recognize
business processes, applications, and databases, along
with technology components—AI systems appear in them,
at best, as a subtype of applications. This classification
underestimates what makes AI different: An AI model
learns from data, behaves probabilistically, and is subject
to Model Drift—its quality gradually declines as reality
diverges from the training data. Therefore, it requires
continuous monitoring.[6] Such an object demands its
own distinct architecture artifacts. The implications of this
are demonstrated by a simple search query: Anyone
wanting to see all systems with personal training data in
the repository will only get a result if training data is
managed there as a distinct entity. Otherwise, the results
list will remain empty, even though the systems exist.
The EA metamodel defines the fundamental
categories and relationships used to describe an enterprise
architecture—such as business capabilities and
15

CTI Research Paper Series • SSR
applications, data, and technology components. As the
"model of models," it determines which architecture
artifacts are documented and governed in the first place.
In this context, First-Class Citizen simply means: a
dedicated category, clearly named attributes, and
regulated maintenance. Five such new building blocks are
added. The first three document what is actually running.
The AI system is established as an independent
architecture component alongside classic applications,
with attributes such as model type, risk category, and the
responsible Business Unit. The AI model includes
versioning and provenance information; moreover,
performance metrics and the lifecycle status are added.[6]
Data lineage—the documented trail of data from its source
to the finished model—becomes an explicit artifact: It
records where training data originates and which quality
metrics it meets.[7] The two remaining building blocks
make the inventory governable. AI risks become
documented categories—Bias, hallucination, or Model
Drift—that are linked to specific systems.[8] Finally, the
model lifecycle becomes a process artifact that maps
training and validation, deployment, and retirement as
governed phases.[6]

C. Federated AI Governance
The marketing department wants the AI-powered
campaign to go live next week; the IT department is asking
about data leakage, model providers, and auditability. This
is what AI governance looks like in daily operations.
Decentralized business teams need short decision-making
paths and access to new tools without lengthy approval
processes; central IT and EA teams champion architectural
consistency, security, and compliance. Both sides are
right, and the conflict is systemic.
At the control level, this corresponds to federated
governance. What Chapter 3 structurally separated -
central platform services with decentralized application
responsibility - becomes the order of responsibilities here.
Standards, platform services, and risk classification
remain central, along with the guardrails themselves. The
business teams retain local autonomy in selecting specific
AI applications - within these very guardrails.[11]
Three mechanisms support this operationally, and the
first is a tiered review process: In place of the monolithic
architecture board, there is a review depth that depends on
the risk profile. An internal translation tool with no access
to customer data undergoes an automated self-assessment
check; an application classified as high-risk requires a full
architecture review, including a compliance check.[10]
The governance scales with the risk.
The second mechanism replaces gatekeeping with
guardrails. The platform provides standardized, secure
building blocks with which business departments can
experiment freely within defined limits.[4] An LLM
gateway, for example, opens up access to various language
models, while automatically filtering sensitive data and
logging usage. The guardrail is embedded in the
infrastructure.
Governance as Code, introduced in Chapter 3 as an
architectural principle, ultimately shifts policies from
documents to automated policies on the AI platform.[6] A
new AI model then automatically undergoes bias checks,
performance validations, and compliance reviews before it
can be deployed to production. If a threshold is breached,
deployment is halted without anyone having to write an
approval email. The checks are integrated into the MLOps
pipeline, i.e., in the automated build and rollout track for
models.
Formal processes alone rarely sustain the balance
between exploration and exploitation. Alongside these, a
softer layer is at work: the informal networks, the
communities of practice, through which central and
decentralized teams actually communicate with each
other. Ambidexterity research examines this interplay of
formal and personal coordination mechanisms in
individual executives; it finds positive correlations for
both.[12] For the governance level, this is an analogy, not
proof - but one that is regularly confirmed in practice.

D. New Roles and Structures
Without a clear mandate, AI governance ends up
somewhere between the architecture function, the Data
Office, Security, and Legal—in other words, nowhere.
More recent conceptual works describe the Enterprise AI
Architect for this purpose: a bridging role between the
business department and IT, data responsibility, and
compliance.[4] This role combines architectural thinking
with robust knowledge of AI technologies, data strategies,
and regulatory requirements.
The Enterprise AI Architect is responsible for the AI
reference architecture and sets the standards for model
development and deployment. This person evaluates new
AI technologies for architectural fit and ensures that AI
initiatives are integrated into the overall architecture.[9]
Their guiding question is not, “How do I train the best
model?” but rather, “How does this model fit into the
three-layer architecture, the governance structures, and the
compliance requirements?”

E. Regulation as a Catalyst
For architectural work, what the frameworks are called is
less important than the evidence they require. Three
currently shape the approach to AI risks, with very
different degrees of binding force: the EU AI Act as
applicable law, ISO/IEC 42001 as a voluntarily certifiable
international standard for AI management systems, and
the NIST AI Risk Management Framework as a voluntary

However, this role does not make decisions alone. For
risk classification, budgeting, and prioritization, the
organization needs an AI Governance Board with a clear
mandate, staffed cross-functionally to reflect the cross-
sectional nature of AI. The board is chaired by the CIO or
the CDO; seated at the table are the relevant Business
Units, Legal and Compliance, and the Data Lead. Security
is represented twice—once through information security
(CISO or the person responsible for IT security) and once
through Security Architecture.
Especially for Enterprise AI, Security Architecture
belongs on the Governance Board as a formative role, not
as a subordinate control function. It assesses access
patterns as well as provider and third-party risks, secure
designs for Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and
LLM gateways, in addition to guardrail architectures and
auditability. Thus, it connects Security by Design with the
architectural principles from Chapter 3 and prevents
security from becoming a roadblock only when going live.

For high-risk systems, it requires a risk management

2, 2025, and the transparency obligations under Article 50

Anyone who concludes from this that the topic has been
deferred underestimates the scale of the task. Data lineage
across organically grown system landscapes, a complete
AI system register, and robust audit trails are not created in
a single quarter. The time gained is time for building, not a
deferral.
ISO/IEC 42001:2023 supplements the legal framework
with a management system-oriented approach. The
overlap lies less in the terminology than in the subject
matter: Where the EU AI Act requires the origin of
training data to be documented, the standard introduces its
own control for data provenance; where it demands
automatic event logging, the standard specifies the
recording of system events.[15] The NIST AI Risk
Management Framework structures the handling of AI
risks into four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, and
Manage.[16]
Consequently, the regulation demands evidence that a
viable architecture should generate anyway. However, it
does not mandate an AI system register for companies that
merely use AI—the registration under Article 49 applies to
providers, and on the operator side, only to public
authorities. Nevertheless, there is no way around an
inventory: AI literacy under Article 4, compliance with the
prohibitions under Article 5, oversight and log retention
under Article 26, and the assessment of whether one
becomes a provider oneself through a substantial
modification all presuppose that the organization knows
its own inventory.[17] Moreover, documented data
lineage makes troubleshooting and fine-tuning traceable,
and audit trails provide the transparency for effective
Model Lifecycle Management.[8]
This suggests that regulation should not become the
sole driver of governance investments. Organizations
pursuing governance for compliance reasons alone tend to
produce bureaucratic shells without operational value.
Value appears to arise only where compliance
requirements are translated into architectural
requirements.
Enterprise Architecture is the discipline that can
provide the architectural evidence that regulators expect.
[4] The three-layer architecture from Chapter 3 forms the
technical foundation: The Data & Knowledge Layer
provides data lineage, the AI Platform Layer documents
model lifecycles, and the Application Layer captures the
integration into business processes. The governance layer
permeates all three levels as a cross-cutting function.[6]
An organization that has established this mapping cleanly
can answer an audit request from its inventory rather than
from memory.
The three frameworks converge on largely the same
items of evidence, even if they name them differently: an
overview of the systems in use, a risk or impact
assessment, the origin of the data, and event logging.
Therefore, an organization that builds them once
architecturally can serve them largely in common, instead
of maintaining a separate evidence trail for each
framework. This is the economic core of this investment,
alongside better governance and scalability. When the
obligations for high-risk systems take effect, companies
1

must provide evidence that cannot be delivered without
architectural anchoring.
A metamodel and governance, roles and regulation do
not yet create a productive platform. What becomes of it in
practice is decided by the sequence: the steps in which it is
built and the result one step must deliver before the next
can be supported.
According to the position of this paper, Enterprise
Architecture Management is the natural home for
Enterprise AI Governance because, as a central discipline,
it establishes the link between business strategy,
technology landscape, and compliance. Whether an
organization truly fulfills this role is shown by three
sobering questions: Does the AI system register have an
owner who keeps it up-to-date? Does the Governance
Board meet monthly? Is at least one guideline available as
an executable policy on the platform instead of as a
document on the intranet? Where all three answers are
missing, EAM remains a documentation function for AI as
well—and the governance gap described in this chapter
remains open.

Enterprise AI transformation follows a six-stage phase
model—from strategic analysis to continuous
optimization—that is traversed iteratively and not
processed linearly. Quick wins and platform investments
run in parallel, in a conscious dual-track strategy: Every
quick value contribution builds upon the emerging
platform. The ROI argumentation for AI platforms
demands new logics beyond project ROI: Total Cost of
Ownership, option value, risk reduction, and time-to-
value for subsequent projects constitute the relevant key
metrics.

A. Phase Model of Enterprise AI Transformation
Companies that manage the transition from pilots to a
platform go through six phases.[1] They build on one
another without being strictly sequential: in practice, they
overlap, and setbacks are normal. More important than the
sequence is what results a phase must deliver before the
next one becomes viable. The phase durations mentioned
are consistently based on experience from transformation
projects, not empirically collected mean values.

Without transparency about the current state, the basis for

building the future target architecture on a foundation that
does not exist.

defines the target architectural structure, decides between
a central, federated, and hybrid approach, and specifies the
three layers for the specific corporate context.[4] The as-is

platform development plan with clear milestones. The
interplay between the business department, data science,
and operations must also be planned; a significant portion
of the friction in ML projects arises at these interfaces.[5]
Prioritizing here also means leaving things behind.
Organizations that want to implement everything at once

A significant risk factor lies between Phase 4 and Phase

rule of thumb derived from transformation projects has
proven serviceable here: the first productive use case
should be live within 90 days of project start; this is not an
empirically determined threshold. Organizations that
substantially exceed this timeframe risk a loss of
management sponsorship and organizational attention.
Phase 5: Implementation (3-9 months). The
implementation phase iteratively builds the AI platform
and, in parallel, implements the prioritized use cases.
Chapter 2 described how wide this range is: what can be
achieved in about three months in a medium-sized
business can extend over nine months or longer in a
corporation with a complex legacy landscape. The
development is driven by demand. A platform component
that is not backed by a specific use case remains
infrastructure in reserve.
Phase 6: Continuous Optimization (ongoing). The
sixth phase marks the beginning of continuous operation:
performance monitoring, a growing use case pipeline, and
architectural evolution. AI models are subject to
phenomena like concept drift and therefore require
permanent monitoring.[4]
B. Balancing Quick Wins and Platform
Investments
Quick wins build organizational buy-in and secure funding
for the platform. However, relying solely on them repeats
the mistakes of the past: every isolated quick win created
without a common platform generates a new siloed
solution.
The solution lies in a dual-track strategy. Track 1
identifies use cases with high business value and short
implementation times—typical candidates include the
automation of repetitive back-office processes, AI-
powered knowledge retrieval, or support for customer
interactions. According to the MIT report, back-office
automation, in particular, often achieves a higher return
than the sales and marketing applications that are the focus
of investment.[6] In parallel, Track 2 builds the
architectural foundations—Data Foundation and MLOps
pipeline, along with a governance framework and Model
Registry.
The crucial design principle is: Every quick win must
run on the emerging platform, not alongside it.
Organizations that successfully manage this transition
define a minimal platform core as early as Phase 3, upon
which all quick wins are built. Productive ML platforms
have demonstrated for years that recurring building blocks
—data preparation, model management, deployment—are
worthwhile as shared components.[7] In our experience, a
central LLM gateway is added for generative applications.

The cadence is a deliberate decision. In the first 90
days, the emphasis is on Track 1—proving that AI
generates tangible business value. From the third month
onward, the focus increasingly shifts to Track 2. By month
six, the platform should be sufficiently established for new
use cases to be onboarded noticeably faster; this is
precisely its value proposition compared to a series of
individual projects.
C. ROI Argumentation for Platform Investments
Traditional ROI calculations measure the return of a single
project against its costs. For platforms, this falls short:
their value only emerges from the many use cases that are
built on them over time.[8] Therefore, different key figures
are needed when communicating with the CFO.
Total Cost of Ownership (TCO). Consolidation on a
platform lowers the total costs compared to decentralized
tool sprawl. Chapter 2, with figures from the Torii survey,
has shown how extensive a company's application
landscape is on average and what proportion of it was
procured without IT's involvement.[9] Every additional
siloed AI solution adds to this tally - with licenses and
integration at the beginning, and with maintenance and
governance in the long run.
Option Value. An AI platform creates the option to
realize future use cases quickly and cost-effectively. The
first use case rarely justifies the investment on its own;
every subsequent one leverages the existing infrastructure.
Chapter 3 introduced cloud migration as an analogy for
this. In terms of platform economics, this is the standard
case: the value of a platform lies in what is built upon it.
[10]
Risk Reduction. Operationally, the risk decreases
because sanctioned self-service offerings displace Shadow
AI. From a regulatory perspective, it decreases because
compliance requirements can be embedded in the
architecture.[11]
Time-to-Value. The first use case on a new platform
typically takes several months, while subsequent ones take
significantly less time: data integration, model operations,
and approval processes are already in place. This
acceleration is the key figure that supports the platform
approach with the CFO, and it can be measured in-house
as soon as the second and third use cases are running.
Reliable industry benchmarks are not available; internal
measurement replaces them.
D. Success Patterns and Transformation Traps
Four patterns characterize organizations that succeed in
scaling, and four traps characterize those that fail. Three
pairs of these are described: AI as an enterprise-wide
capability versus technical ambition without
organizational preparation (Chapters 3 and 4), federated
governance versus gatekeeping (Chapter 5), and the dual-
track balance versus platform construction without
demand from the business departments (Section 6.2 and
the box in Chapter 3). A trap rarely occurs in isolation.
The fourth success pattern concerns the external
division of labor. The MIT report attributes a rate of
productive deployments to projects with external partners
that is roughly twice as high as that for purely internal
development approaches.[6] Chapters 1 and 2 mention the
reservations regarding this survey.
20

CTI Research Paper Series • SSR
The fourth trap is mentioned less often: Tool selection
takes the place of the architecture decision. Anyone who
starts by comparing LLM providers and MLOps suites lets
the product determine the platform's structure and usually
only notices the lock-in with the second use case that
doesn't fit. The target architecture comes first: It specifies
which capabilities the platform must support and thus
narrows down the pool of candidates. We do not have a
reference in the literature for this; the observation stems
from projects.
E. The Role of External Expertise
Chapter 4 has outlined the contribution of external
experience: Those who know the path recognize the
pitfalls sooner. This is particularly effective in phases 1 to
4; with the implementation, the emphasis shifts inward,
and in continuous operation, it lies there entirely. External
assistance cannot substitute for the development of
internal competence. The architectural governance
capability must emerge within the company itself, and it is
one of the factors that determine the success of digital
transformations.[12] This paper does not establish criteria
for selecting a partner—the authors themselves are
consultants.
F. From the Phase Model to Continuous
Operation
The Enterprise AI Transformation is not complete with the
go-live; it transitions into continuous operation.
Performance monitoring links technical metrics such as
Model Accuracy and Data Drift with business metrics such
as ROI per use case.[4] As technology evolves, the
platform architecture must evolve accordingly.[12]
Moreover, portfolio management maintains the balance
between exploratory use cases, which open up new
possibilities, and exploitative ones, which optimize
existing processes.[8]
The transition is successful when three conditions are
met: The platform is actively used, the governance
structures are functional, and the organization
independently identifies and implements new use cases.
Enterprise AI Transformation follows a structured
phase model, the key to which lies in the dual-track
strategy: Quick wins generate acceptance, while the
platform accelerates every subsequent use case. External
experience shortens the path to this goal; however, it does
not replace internal competence building.

VII. CONCLUSION AND OUTLOOK
The paradox between AI investment and scaling success is
resolved as soon as AI is understood as a matter of
architecture and governance. Enterprise Architecture
Management can assume governance if it moves beyond a
mere documentation role to provide continuous steering—
and if it incorporates the accompanying success factors:
use cases, ownership, data, and others, all the way to
security and compliance. With Agentic AI, the importance
of architectural foundations continues to grow—those
who invest today are creating strategic options for the
next generation of technology.
A. The Paradox - Resolved
Many CIOs are familiar with the observation that opened
this paper from their own organizations: Investments in
Artificial Intelligence are increasing, yet the returns are
falling short of expectations. The preceding chapters lead
to a clear conclusion. The failure of AI initiatives is neither
a coincidence nor purely a technology problem. Often, it is
the direct, predictable result of a lack of architectural
anchoring. This is compounded by unclear prioritization,
weak ownership, and a lack of change capability, as well
as non-integrated security and compliance requirements.
The four anti-patterns—Shadow AI, Pilot Purgatory,
Governance Vacuum, and Data Quality Deficits—are
symptoms of a common deficit: the lack of architectural
anchoring for AI within the enterprise landscape. Chapter
2 diagnosed these patterns, while Chapter 3 developed the
architectural responses. Thus, the initial paradox is
resolved. Technology is not the primary constraint on AI
success. What is missing are the structures to embed, scale,
and govern it—including the organizational, security, and
regulatory prerequisites required to sustainably support
productive AI systems.

B. Three Messages for Practice
First: AI scaling is primarily a matter of architectural
thinking. Companies that successfully scale AI treat it as
an Enterprise Capability within a well-conceived platform
landscape. The three-layer architecture provides the
orientation for this transformation by separating data
management, model operations, and application
integration into manageable areas of responsibility.
Architectural thinking implies that every AI initiative is
integrated into an overall structure from the first day, even
where that structure initially consists of no more than a
deliberate commitment to three architectural principles.
Months of preliminary work on a target architecture are
not a prerequisite.
Second: Enterprise Architecture Management is the
central discipline with the cross-functional mandate
for this task. EAM connects business strategy, AI
technology, and data, as well as security and compliance—
but only as a Dynamic Capability. This role demands a
transformation. AI becomes a First-Class Citizen in the
metamodel, the central gatekeeper gives way to federated
governance, and the static blueprint yields to continuous
adaptation. The empirical validation of this connection—
does the maturity level of Enterprise Architecture
Management show a measurable effect on AI scaling
success?—is a research desideratum that this paper
formulates as a hypothesis and recommends for future
investigation.
Third: The journey begins with the next step. The
phase model in Chapter 6 shows a structured path from
strategic analysis to a productive platform. The dual-track
strategy resolves the apparent contradiction between
short-term expectations for results and long-term
architectural investment: Quick Wins on Track 1, platform
development on Track 2. Crucially, every Quick Win runs
on the emerging platform.
C. Outlook - why architecture is becoming more
important now
The foreseeable development towards Agentic AI and
multi-agent systems increases the architectural
requirements. Chapter 4 evaluated the Gartner forecast on
this topic: a surge in proliferation and the abandonment
rate stand side-by-side in the same analysis.[1]
Expectation and viability are diverging. This is the same
pattern that this paper has described for the AI wave to
date.
Agentic AI intensifies the requirements in several
respects: Autonomous agents require more robust
guardrails than assisting systems. Multi-agent
architectures generate more complex interaction patterns.
Moreover, the requirements for Observability increase
when AI systems transition from recommending to acting.
The hidden technical debt, which Chapter 3 introduced
with Sculley et al.,[2] is particularly impactful in multi-
agent architectures because the incorrect decision of one
agent propagates to others. All of this presupposes the
architectural foundations that this paper describes.
The paradigm shift is already emerging: AI is evolving
from a tool into an actor. This transformation requires
more architecture. Those who invest in platform

D. The Call to Action
Better models, higher budgets, and more pilots do not
close the gap between AI investment and AI value
creation. What is often missing is the architectural
integration that brings use cases, platform, and data into a
viable overall structure—including Governance, Security,
and Change.
The first concrete step is to establish transparency over
the current AI landscape. An AI system registry can be
established in a few weeks, immediately generates
information relevant for governance, and creates the
foundation for any further governance measures. This is
followed by a target architecture based on the three layers,
conceived as a concrete organizational framework for
current and future initiatives. Finally, federated
governance structures with clear role assignments are
implemented.
According to the MIT report, those who work with
external partners during scaling are significantly more
likely to achieve productive deployment than those who go
it alone.[3] Chapter 2 mentions the reservations about the
survey; however, the direction aligns with the experience
that initial runs are more expensive than repetitions.
The coming one to two years may be characterized as a
favorable window of opportunity for establishing
foundational AI architecture. Technological maturity,
regulatory requirements, and organizational readiness are
currently converging, and the postponed deadlines of the
EU AI Act afford the time required to build robust chains
of evidence.
The paradox from Chapter 1 has a resolution: The
money is often tied to the wrong investment logic. Those
who invest in technology while neglecting architecture,
ownership, and the data foundation create fragmentation;
the same applies to security and the capacity for change.
Conversely, those who invest in architecture and build
technology upon it generate scalable value creation. The
difference is decided in mindset before it is reflected in the
budget.
AI scaling is primarily an architectural and governance
decision, not purely a technological one. The three-layer
architecture, Security by Design, and federated
governance form the foundation for the journey from
pilots to platforms—supported by an EAM in a governing
role. In the coming wave of Agentic AI, these foundations
will determine competitiveness.
